# Track record: what is verified, what is self-reported, and what could not be confirmed

*Research compiled 13 November 2026 (Asia/Shanghai). Every figure below is attributed. Nothing here is an audited recreation of Camillo's books. See [DISCLAIMER.md](../DISCLAIMER.md).*

Chris Camillo is a self-taught American investor, the author of *Laughing at Wall Street* (St. Martin's Press, 2011), a co-founder of the social-data firm TickerTags, and a co-host of the YouTube channel and podcast Dumb Money (with Dave Hanson and Jordan McLain). He describes his method as **social arbitrage**: observing everyday consumer, cultural, and technological change, connecting it to a public company, and investing before Wall Street prices the change in. Jack Schwager's chapter on him in *Unknown Market Wizards* (2020) is titled "Neither," because the method is neither fundamental nor technical analysis.

The popular origin story — "$20,000 to $2 million in three years," later stretched to "$42 million," "$70 million," or "$80 million" — mixes **independently reviewed brokerage records**, **self-reported later totals**, and **inconsistent account definitions**. The honest way to read the record is to keep those layers apart.

## 1. The independently reviewed core

### Schwager and Business Insider, 2006–2021

The most careful public audit is Jack D. Schwager's review of monthly brokerage statements, first for *Unknown Market Wizards* and then updated for Business Insider.

In the 18 August 2021 Business Insider feature by Laila Maidan:

- Schwager reviewed monthly statements **beginning in August 2006**. Copies of those statements were also provided to Insider.
- The **starting balance was $84,000**.
- By **May 2020** the account was **over $21 million**, *including cumulative net profits he had withdrawn*.
- The average annual **compounded return from 2006 to 2020 was 77%**. Over the same window the S&P 500 compounded at **7.6%**, per the article.
- Schwager **updated the audit for that article** and found that Camillo had **doubled his cumulative profits, to $42 million, by May 2021**.
- Insider itself **viewed Form 1099 brokerage documents from 2014 to 2019** that showed **gains of about $5.6 million**.
- At Insider's request, **Biljana Adebambo**, academic director of the M.S. in Finance at the University of San Diego School of Business, reviewed the same statements and **agreed with Schwager's assessment**. She also flagged the risk: the result "likely required some use of riskier strategies such as options," and "volatility of the portfolio from inception was almost **five times** as large as that of a S&P 500 ETF over the same period." That level of risk, she wrote, "may not be acceptable to many investors, ex ante."

Camillo later wrote on LinkedIn that Business Insider had spent **three months independently auditing 15 years of trade statements** before publishing.

An earlier Insider piece (Christopher Competiello, 7 December 2020) reported two additional statement-backed figures:

- From February 2020, as the pandemic hit, the portfolio had "swelled by **over $9.7 million**," on a statement "seen by Business Insider and vetted by Schwager."
- "Since 2006, Camillo has generated **over $16.5 million** in profits."

Those December 2020 profit totals sit **below** the May 2020 "over $21 million including withdrawals" figure in the 2021 article. The two articles are measuring slightly different things (profits versus account value including withdrawals; different cut-off dates). We do **not** force them into one number. Both were described as statement-backed.

### The 2011 accountant's audit, as Camillo described it

In a 12 December 2011 Tearsheet / Tradestreaming Radio interview, Camillo said his portfolio had been "fully audited by an accounting firm," published on laughingatwallstreet.com or chriscamillo.com, and that he believed he was "the only author of financial books in history to have my investments audited." He then drew a distinction that later headlines usually drop:

> "While it doesn't audit the big money segment of my account, which went from $20,000 to $2 million, it just audits my entire account, which I think went from **$67,000 to $2.4 million** over that three and a half year period."

So, **in his own 2011 telling**, the accountant's report covers the **whole account**, not the isolated "$20k sleeve," and the whole-account path he recalled was **$67,000 to $2.4 million** over about three and a half years (roughly 2007–2010).

Wikipedia, citing a PDF labelled "Chris Camilo Audit," says a 2014 independent accountant's report covered **1 December 2006 through 30 November 2013** and documented **seven years of 84% average portfolio returns**. We were **not able to retrieve that PDF** for this archive. The 84% / seven-year figure is therefore **secondary** (Wikipedia and promotional biographies such as Chart Academy, which rounds it to "over 80%"). It is **consistent in spirit** with the later Schwager 77% CAGR, but it is not a document we re-examined.

### What "starting capital" actually was

This is the most commonly confused fact in the Camillo literature.

| Claim | Period | Number | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "I started with $20,000" | Often dated 2007; book era 2007–2010 | $20,000 → "just over $2 million" | **Self-described**, including in the book, US News, Bloomberg, and later interviews. In 2011 he said the accountant did *not* isolate this sleeve. |
| Accountant's whole account, per the 2011 interview | ~3.5 years ending ~2010 | **$67,000 → $2.4 million** | **Self-described summary** of an audit we could not re-retrieve. |
| Schwager / Business Insider | Statements from **August 2006** | **$84,000** starting balance → **>$21 million** by May 2020 (incl. withdrawals); **$42 million** cumulative profits by May 2021 | **Independently reviewed** by Schwager; statements also viewed by Insider and by Prof. Adebambo. |
| Later interviews (VladTV and others, c. 2026) | "17 years" | Original $20,000 has "generated about **$80 million** of returns"; "I think my audit shows about **68% annualized** over seventeen years" | **Self-reported**. We found no second-party audit of the $80 million or the 68% figure. |
| Chart Academy instructor page (undated, accessed 2026) | — | "more than **$70 million**"; "over 80% returns a year for seven years" | **Promotional**, not an audit. |
| CMC Markets / Opto Sessions (1 Sep 2022) | — | "turning $20,000 into more than **$30 million**" | **Journalistic paraphrase**, inconsistent with the May 2021 $42 million *cumulative profits* figure; likely a different definition (account value vs. lifetime profits). |

Two compatible readings, neither of which we can prove beyond the sources: he may have begun a high-risk sleeve with about $20,000 around 2007, while the **brokerage relationship Schwager audited already held about $84,000 in August 2006**; and he has **added and withdrawn capital** over the years, so "account value" and "cumulative trading profits" diverge. Complete Traders Edge (August 2026) makes that point and correctly treats the Schwager record as the anchor. We agree.

Camillo has also said, in a 2026 interview, that if he had left the money in the portfolio instead of withdrawing it for taxes, lifestyle, and startup investing, the $20,000 "maybe" would be worth **$700 million**. That is a **hypothetical self-estimate**, not a track record.

## 2. The book, Covestor, and the early public story

*Laughing at Wall Street: How I Beat the Pros at Investing (by Reading Tabloids, Shopping at the Mall, and Connecting on Facebook) and How You Can, Too* was published by St. Martin's Press in November 2011 (ISBN 978-0-312-65785-7). Camillo has said a **book deal followed his ranking as the number-one trader on Covestor** (also spelled Covester in some interviews), a portfolio-tracking service to which, he said, about **40,000** people had connected brokerage accounts. We did **not** independently re-verify the Covestor ranking against Covestor's own archives; it is widely repeated by him and by contemporaneous press, and we treat it as **claimed, consistently, and not contradicted**, but not re-audited here.

Contemporaneous coverage included a US News piece ("How One Amateur Investor Spots Stocks Before Wall Street," Susan Taylor, 1 November 2011), a Bloomberg item titled along the lines of "This Guy Turned $20K Into $2 Million," CNBC, and Fox Business.

Two professional reviews were skeptical in a useful way:

- **Publishers Weekly** (19 September 2011) summarized the $20,000-to-$2 million claim, warned that he "only discusses potential losses at the end of the book when describing options and leverage, risky techniques, which he espouses," noted that he "summarily dismisses both technical and fundamental analysis," and concluded that "the probability that readers can replicate his experience and success is questionable." It also used the word "lucky."
- **Kirkus Reviews** (release date 1 November 2011) called the opening "like a loud, late-night infomercial," then argued that "duplicating that feat" requires "highly technical and complicated financial tools" — call options — that the marketing pitch undersells. "Even if Camillo’s premise is correct, actually capitalizing on Main Street’s alleged entrepreneurial advantage over Wall Street requires a lot more technical expertise than is available in this high-pressure how-to guide."

Those critiques still stand: the **observational edge** and the **options-and-concentration engine** are different things, and popular tellings often advertise the first while the returns require the second.

## 3. Notable trades, with sources and verification status

We looked for the anecdotes that circulate around Camillo — including Pabst and the Vegas Golden Knights, which were suggested as "type" stories — and kept only what we could source. **Trade-level P&L is almost always self-reported**; a few positions were **screenshot-verified** by Business Insider.

### Screenshot-verified or statement-adjacent (Business Insider)

**Dorel Industries (bicycles: Schwinn, Cannondale) — spring–autumn 2020.** Camillo said pandemic closures produced a surge of outdoor and bicycle conversation. Insider **viewed TD Ameritrade screenshots**: he bought on **24 April 2020 at $1.56**. He "gradually sold" in **June near $4.46**, and told Insider he had **fully exited in November**, by which time the price had climbed to **$11.38, a 629% rise**. The 629% is the **stock's** move from his entry to the November price, **not** a verified personal return: he had already been selling in June.

**Restaurant Brands International (QSR) — 2019, a loss.** He told Insider one of his biggest losses was **$750,000** on RBI, a bet on Burger King's Impossible Whopper and Popeyes' chicken sandwich that was undone by Tim Hortons, "one of the worst quarters in its history." He called it "an oversight in not understanding how badly the third piece of the brand was doing," and blamed limited Canadian social data. Insider **viewed screenshots** of purchases in **April and again in August 2019**. The price **peaked at $78.45 in August** and **fell 59% by March 2020**. The **$750,000 loss figure is his statement**; the purchase dates and the stock's path were corroborated by screenshots and market prices.

**Tapestry (TPR) — 2021.** Insider **viewed screenshots** of a **$3.1 million** position across two accounts, initiated after he "identif[ied] a surge in positive social interest in Coach bags," including a TikTok review with about 650,000 views. He framed it as a millennial/Gen-Z "Coach comeback," and listed supply-chain, freight, and China-shutdown risks. This verifies the **position size at the time of the article**, not a closed-trade P&L.

**Playa Hotels & Resorts (PLYA) — 2021 thesis.** In the same 2021 interview he said he was "heavily invested" after Google Trends beach-travel terms hit a **five-year high**, seasonally adjusted. Insider noted Playa's stock was **up about 32% year-to-date through 15 August 2021**. That is a market fact plus a self-described holding, not a closed-trade result.

### Self-reported in interviews, with contemporaneous journalism

**Snapple puts, as a teenager (late 1980s, self-reported).** He has told this story consistently since at least 2011 (Tearsheet) and again to Business Insider (2020) and in 2026 interviews: a 7-Eleven had cut Snapple from two refrigerator doors to half of one; the clerk cited new competition (Arizona and others); his stockbroker brother bought **puts** with the **$300** the younger Camillo had from garage sales; Snapple's next earnings showed inventory building up; he **tripled** the $300 "in a few weeks" (Tearsheet; "three times my money," BI 2020). There is **no independent confirmation** of this childhood trade. It is best read as his **origin myth for the method**, told without material contradiction over 15 years.

**e.l.f. Beauty (ELF) — March 2019.** In the December 2020 Insider interview he said a viral Jeffree Star YouTube review of e.l.f.'s Poreless Putty Primer, followed by Twitter/Instagram chatter and **in-store interviews at Target and pharmacies** (sold out; customers buying the rest of the line), led him to buy. "In the six weeks following Camillo's initial purchase, the stock soared **over 50%**." The **50% is the stock move after his stated entry**, reported by him to a journalist; we did not see a screenshot of the fill. The Jeffree Star video and e.l.f.'s subsequent consumer moment are real cultural facts.

**Target × Missoni — 2011.** In the Tearsheet interview he said coworkers and his wife flagged Target's Missoni designer line; Wall Street was unsure it would land "in the middle of the country." He waited in line, saw a **400-piece line sell out in 90 minutes**, confirmed the same scene on "mommy blogs," fashion blogs, and Twitter, "made a trade in a lawn chair in the back of the store," watched it hit CNBC that afternoon and the Today show the next morning, and was **out by the next morning**, having "**double[d] my money overnight in a leverage-options play**." **Self-reported.** The Missoni-for-Target sell-out is a well-documented retail event; his personal P&L is not independently confirmed.

**Elmer's / Newell Brands (NWL) — the slime trade.** In a CMC Markets / Opto Sessions interview published 1 September 2022, he called this one of his favorites: a global kids' slime craze, white glue as the main ingredient, Elmer's "selling out globally," an investment in parent **Newell Brands**. He said Newell "saw its revenue spike **50%**, despite it only making up **1–2%** of its total revenue," and that he "made **close to 200%** on the leveraged trade." **Self-reported**, including the 50% / 1–2% / 200% figures, which we did not re-audit against Newell's filings for this archive. The cultural fact of the mid-2010s slime craze is not in dispute.

**Crocs (CROX) — a recurring, multi-year involvement.** In August 2021 he told Insider the stock "had tripled over the past year, and I'm still adding," citing celebrity endorsements and a new double-strap sandal. In a 3 August 2022 Benzinga / Yahoo Finance interview he said he had been investing in Crocs "**for a decade on and off**," that "the market has generally always hated Crocs" and treated it as "the longest lasting fad ever," and that he was still long, bullish on the **HeyDude** acquisition ($2.5 billion, December 2021) as a case of "**geographical arbitrage**" (a southern-state cult brand Wall Street could miss). He did **not** give a closed-trade lifetime P&L in that interview; "strong returns over the years trading in and out" is **self-described**. Crocs' pandemic-era fundamental improvement is a matter of public financials.

**COVID stay-at-home and outdoor basket, 2020.** In the December 2020 Insider interview he named **Peloton, Amazon, Shopify, and "outdoor stocks"** as names that "should never have gone down." The **$9.7 million** portfolio rise from February 2020 is the statement-vetted figure above; **allocation and per-name P&L were not published**. In later talks he has referred to a stay-at-home basket making him "more than $30 million during COVID" (podcast chapter summaries). That **$30 million** figure is **self-reported in later talks**, and should not be confused with the $9.7 million statement-backed number from 2020.

**Amazon earnings options.** In several 2025–2026 talks (summarized by podcast indexes such as Snipd and My First Million) he describes a high-conviction **Amazon options** trade around earnings, with figures circulating as "**$10 million**" in a day and a story about putting the trade on with the account "down almost 50%." We treat the **$10 million Amazon figure as self-reported** unless and until brokerage statements are published. We did not find a Business Insider-style audit of that single trade.

**Beacon Roofing / "roof repair" Google Trends.** In the same later talks he describes using a spike in Google searches for "roof repair" after hail, ahead of delayed insurance claims, to get to **Beacon Roofing** before hedge funds relying on slower data. **Self-reported**; we found no third-party P&L.

## 4. TickerTags, Dumb Money, and the public platform

**TickerTags.** Camillo has said he co-founded the firm in the mid-2010s (Business Insider: 2015; Wikipedia: launched 2015 after two years of development). It mapped "a million and a half word combinations" (his 2026 interview wording) of social conversation to brands and investable themes, and sold the data to hedge funds and banks. He has said he spent about **five years** in Manhattan training desks on conversational data, and that many funds "couldn't wrap their head around actually making trades off of a data set that didn't have years of historical correlation." TickerTags was widely reported as **among the first social-data shops to call a Leave result in the 2016 Brexit referendum** (Wikipedia cites a contemporaneous "TickerTags First to Call Brexit Results Using Twitter Data" item). **M Science**, the alternative-data firm in the Jefferies Financial Group orbit, **acquired TickerTags in June 2018** (Business Wire, 1 June 2018; also Integrity Research and a WSJ story on social media in hedge-fund research, June 2018). Camillo posted at the time that he was "excited for the future of TickerTags under the new leadership of M Science."

**Dumb Money.** In 2018, Camillo, **Dave Hanson**, and **Jordan McLain** left day jobs to invest full-time and launched the YouTube channel (Dumb Money / Dumb Money Live / DumbMoney.tv) and a podcast, plus a Discord where, Camillo has said, many of his later ideas are surfaced. CMC Markets wrote in September 2022 that the channel had about **50,000** subscribers when Camillo first spoke to them in October 2020 and had "more than doubled" by 2022. Follower counts move; treat any current number as dated the day you check YouTube, X, or Stocktwits. As of sources consulted in 2026, third-party trackers put @ChrisCamillo on X in the **~400,000** range and the YouTube channel in the **~200,000** subscriber range. We did not treat those snapshots as part of his investment record.

**Stocktwits / Twitter claims.** Camillo has used Stocktwits and X as public notebooks. Viral posts and "I called this" threads are **self-published**. They are useful as a window on timing and process; they are **not** a second audit.

**Other background**, from his own interviews and a Capital Factory mentor profile (cited on Wikipedia): early jobs in a Dean Witter cold-calling room, a youth program at Fidelity, a failed stint in Los Angeles (talent agency, car sales), then a Texas sales career before the 2007–2010 run; later early-stage investing (he has said "180" companies, with "about 10%" returns — **self-reported**). Capital Factory lists exits including Research Now, eCarList, Order My Gear, and TickerTags. Those operating career facts are **not** part of the trading audit.

## 5. Critiques, risk, and an honest reading of "success"

1. **Leverage and volatility are load-bearing.** Adebambo's review is the cleanest academic comment we have: outstanding performance, almost certainly options-assisted, with **~5× the volatility** of an S&P 500 ETF. Kirkus and Publishers Weekly said the same in 2011, in plainer language. Any retelling that offers "just look at TikTok" as a sufficient explanation of a 77% CAGR is incomplete.
2. **Survivorship and selection.** Schwager's *Market Wizards* franchise profiles unusual winners. One documented path does not imply that "social arbitrage," taught at scale through YouTube and Discord, produces similar paths for students. Complete Traders Edge (2026) makes this point, and we agree.
3. **Self-report inflation at the headline layer.** The audited anchor is **77% CAGR, 2006–2020, $84,000 starting, $42 million cumulative profits by May 2021.** Later "$70 million" and "$80 million" totals, "68% over 17 years," and "$30 million on COVID" or "$10 million on Amazon" are **his later words**, useful as claims, not as a second Schwager letter. Different journalists have also paraphrased different lifetime totals ($30 million in 2022, $42 million in 2021, $80 million in 2026) without always specifying definition and date.
4. **The $20,000 origin is a sleeve, or at least not the Schwager starting balance.** Treating "$20k to $80 million" as a single compounded account is **misleading**. He has been explicit, including in 2011, that money was withdrawn, that a "big money" sleeve sat inside a larger account, and that capital was added.
5. **Not every observation is an edge, and he says so.** Silicon Image, Restaurant Brands / Tim Hortons, and the 2025 Lululemon Trends false positive are part of the record. He has also said he "miss[es] way more investment opportunities than I catch."
6. **"I almost never go wrong" (2011) should be read with care.** In Tearsheet he said the method "almost never goes wrong" because he makes only one or two investments a year. That is a statement about **selection frequency**, not a claim that a high percentage of all human-copycat trades will work. Later he described "80-ish high conviction bets… on 80 different narratives over 17 years" — a higher pace than "one or two a year," and another reminder that his own description of activity has evolved.
7. **Privacy and legality footnotes.** The 2011 medical-study anecdote (patients posting from inside a trial) raises ethics questions the interview itself only brushes ("it's public information"). We record it; we do not recommend imitating grey-area information gathering.

## Bottom line on the record

There is a **real, independently reviewed**, high-return, high-volatility track record: Schwager and Business Insider, with a second academic reader, on monthly statements from August 2006, **77% compounded through 2020**, **$42 million cumulative profits by May 2021**, starting from **$84,000**, with 1099s for 2014–2019 showing about **$5.6 million** of gains in those years alone. That is rare, and it is not a rumor.

Around that core sits a **larger cloud of self-reported lifetime totals, single-trade P&Ls, and marketing numbers**. They may be honest; they are not the same as the audit. A serious student should memorize the audited core, study the **method** in the next note, and refuse to treat "$20k to $80 million by reading TikTok" as a literal, apples-to-apples account path.
